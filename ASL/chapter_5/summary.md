# Chapter 5: Linear Regression — Least Squares & the Normal Equations — Summary

> **Source:** Lecture 5 side notes. This chapter makes the abstract risk minimization of Chapter 4 **concrete** for the linear model: it derives the *closed-form* least-squares estimator, connects the regression function to the **conditional expectation**, and reviews what an **expectation** actually is.

---

## 1. The Linear Model with Gaussian Noise

We assume the data is generated by a linear function plus random noise:

$$y = \beta_0 + \mathbf{x}^\top \boldsymbol\beta + \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$$

- $\beta_0$ = **intercept** (bias term).
- $\boldsymbol\beta = (\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_p)^\top$ = **slope coefficients** (one per feature).
- $\varepsilon$ = **error / noise term**, here assumed **Gaussian** with mean $0$ and variance $\sigma^2$.

We collect all parameters into a single vector:

$$\boldsymbol\theta = (\beta_0, \boldsymbol\beta^\top)^\top$$

> The Gaussian-noise assumption is exactly the one that makes the **L2 (squared) loss** the maximum-likelihood loss (see Chapter 4: *Gaussian errors ⟺ L2-loss*).

---

## 2. The Regression Function = Conditional Expectation

Take the conditional expectation of $y$ given $\mathbf{x}$. Because $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0$:

$$\mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}] = \beta_0 + \mathbf{x}^\top \boldsymbol\beta = f(\mathbf{x})$$

> **Key idea:** the function we want to learn, $f(\mathbf{x})$, is the **conditional mean** of $y$ given $\mathbf{x}$. This matches Chapter 4's result that the L2-optimal prediction is $\mathbb{E}[y \mid \mathbf{x}]$.

---

## 3. Augmented (Homogeneous) Notation

To absorb the intercept into the dot product, **augment** the feature vector and parameter vector with a leading $1$:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = (1, \mathbf{x}^\top)^\top, \qquad \tilde{\boldsymbol\beta} = (\beta_0, \boldsymbol\beta^\top)^\top$$

Then the model is written compactly as:

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}^\top \tilde{\boldsymbol\beta}$$

Stacking all $n$ augmented observations row-by-row gives the **design matrix** $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (p+1)}$ (first column all ones), and predictions for the whole dataset are $\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta$.

---

## 4. The Least-Squares Objective

With the L2 loss, the empirical risk is the **sum of squared residuals**, written as a squared Euclidean norm:

$$\lVert \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta \rVert_2^2 = (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta)^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta)$$

This is **Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)**. We minimize it over $\boldsymbol\theta$.

---

## 5. Analytical Solution — The Normal Equations

Unlike the general losses of Chapter 4 (Huber, $\epsilon$-insensitive, ...) that needed numerical optimization, OLS has a **closed-form (analytical) solution**.

Expand and differentiate w.r.t. $\boldsymbol\theta$:

$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol\theta}\, (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta)^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta) = -2\,\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta) \stackrel{!}{=} 0$$

Setting the gradient to zero gives the **normal equations**:

$$\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\, \boldsymbol\theta = \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}$$

Solving for $\boldsymbol\theta$ (assuming $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ is invertible):

$$\boxed{\;\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}\;}$$

**Conditions & remarks:**
- $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ is invertible $\iff$ $\mathbf{X}$ has **full column rank** (features linearly independent, $n \ge p+1$).
- The objective is **convex** (L2 loss + linear-in-$\boldsymbol\theta$ model) → this single stationary point is the **global minimum**.
- When $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ is singular / ill-conditioned (e.g. collinear features), the inverse does not exist or is unstable → motivates **regularization** (ridge: $(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \lambda \mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$).

---

## 6. What Is an Expectation? Sample Mean → Theoretical Mean

The estimator above relies on the idea of an **average**. The empirical (sample) mean approximates the **"real" theoretical mean** as $n$ grows (Law of Large Numbers):

$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = \bar{x} \;\longrightarrow\; \mathbb{E}[X]$$

The **theoretical mean** (expectation) is defined by integrating/summing over the distribution:

- **Discrete random variable:**
$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{j=1}^{k} j \cdot \mathbb{P}(X = j)$$

- **Continuous random variable** (density $f$):
$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_{\mathbb{R}} x\, f(x)\, dx$$

- **Joint (vector) case** over $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$:
$$\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{x}, y)] = \int_{\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}} (\mathbf{x}, y)\, f(\mathbf{x}, y)\, d(\mathbf{x}, y)$$

> **Why it matters:** the empirical risk is a sample average that estimates the true (theoretical) risk $\mathbb{E}[L(y, f(\mathbf{x}))]$ — exactly the discrete-sum / continuous-integral object above.

---

## 7. Absolute Loss (L1) — Point-wise Error Recap

For a **new** observation, the absolute prediction error is:

$$\lvert y_i^{\text{new}} - \hat f(\mathbf{x}_i^{\text{new}}) \rvert$$

This is the **L1 / absolute loss**, $g(x) = \lvert x \rvert$:

- A **V-shaped** function with a kink (corner) at $0$ → **not differentiable** at $0$.
- More **robust to outliers** than L2 (error grows linearly, not quadratically).
- Optimal constant model = **median** (vs. mean for L2).

A fitted regression curve $\hat f$ passes through the "middle" of the noisy data; for a query point $\mathbf{x}^{\text{new}}$ the prediction is $\hat f(\mathbf{x}^{\text{new}})$, and the residual is the vertical gap between the true $y^{\text{new}}$ and the curve.

---

## Quick-Check: Common True/False Traps

- Model: $y = \beta_0 + \mathbf{x}^\top\boldsymbol\beta + \varepsilon$ with **Gaussian** noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$.
- Because $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0$, the regression function is the **conditional expectation** $\mathbb{E}[y\mid\mathbf{x}] = f(\mathbf{x})$.
- Augmented notation: $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=(1,\mathbf{x}^\top)^\top$, $f(\mathbf{x})=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^\top\tilde{\boldsymbol\beta}$ — the leading $1$ absorbs the **intercept**.
- OLS objective = $\lVert \mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta\rVert_2^2 = (\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta)^\top(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta)$.
- **Normal equations:** $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$.
- **Closed-form OLS estimator:** $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$ — **analytical**, no iteration needed.
- Needs $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ **invertible** (full column rank); convex problem → the solution is the **global** minimum.
- Sample mean $\bar x \to \mathbb{E}[X]$; discrete $\sum j\,\mathbb{P}(X=j)$, continuous $\int x f(x)\,dx$.
- **L1 / absolute loss** $\lvert y - \hat f(\mathbf{x})\rvert$: robust, **not differentiable at 0**, optimal constant = **median**.
- **Gaussian noise ⟺ L2-loss** → OLS *is* the maximum-likelihood fit under Gaussian errors.
